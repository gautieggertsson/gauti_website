The line at the top of every page is my name, **Gauti B. Eggertsson**, written in runes. Runes were the letters of Scandinavia before the Latin alphabet arrived with Christianity. They were carved rather than written, on stone, bone, and wood, which is why nearly every rune is built from straight strokes that cut easily across the grain.

### The settlement of Iceland

In the early twelfth century, Ari Þorgilsson, known as Ari fróði, wrote *Íslendingabók* (*The Book of the Icelanders*). He names Ingólfur Arnarson as Iceland’s first Norse settler and tells of Christian hermits, the *papar*, who were there before the Norse. The traditional date of 874 comes from *Landnámabók*. When people first arrived and how permanent settlement began are questions historians and archaeologists still debate. [Ari’s account and notes on the dating](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/IslKr.pdf) · [Archaeological dating of the settlement](https://iris.hi.is/en/publications/a-bayesian-approach-to-linking-archaeological-paleoenvironmental-/)

The alphabet in use across Scandinavia at the time of the Norse settlement was the **Younger Futhark**, a compact set of sixteen runes that had taken shape around the year 800. Sixteen signs were not enough for every sound. One rune served for both *k* and *g*, one for *i* and *e*, one for *u* and *o*. Around the eleventh and twelfth centuries scribes began adding a small dot to a rune to mark the second sound, and these **dotted runes** became the standard medieval alphabet in Iceland and Norway. They survive on Icelandic gravestones and church objects well into the fifteenth century. The header uses this medieval form, because it is the one that can spell my name in full.

### What does Gauti mean?

In the final stanza of *Grímnismál*, a poem in the *Poetic Edda*, Óðinn calls himself *Gautr*. My name, *Gauti*, is a variant of *Gautur*, the modern Icelandic form of *Gautr*.

*Gautr* is also one of Óðinn’s names in the *Prose Edda*, written by Snorri Sturluson, a thirteenth-century Icelandic writer and chieftain. [*Gylfaginning*, chapter 20](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/VSNR_Edda-1_prologue_gylfa.pdf)

### My ancestry

Every European alive today descends from Charlemagne. This is not a boast but a theorem. Joseph Chang, a statistician at Yale, showed in 1999 that in a population where people marry more or less at random, the most recent common ancestor of everyone alive lies only a few dozen generations back, and that a little further back every person who left any descendants at all is an ancestor of everyone alive today. The argument is arithmetic. Each of us has two parents, four grandparents, and after thirty generations over a billion ancestral slots, far more than the number of people who lived in Europe at the time. The slots must be filled by the same people many times over, and soon by everyone who had children whose lines survived. Douglas Rohde, Steve Olson and Chang confirmed this with realistic models of geography and migration in *Nature* in 2004. Charlemagne, born in 748, had documented descendants, so he is an ancestor of all of us, most of us by millions of separate paths. The same is true of any ninth-century European who left a family.

What makes Icelanders somewhat peculiar is not the descent. Chang gives that to everyone for free. It is that the descent is written down. Icelanders have been obsessed with documentation from the beginning. Ari fróði wrote *Íslendingabók* around 1130. *Landnáma* recorded the settlers, their farms and their children. The sagas open with genealogies before anything happens. The church kept registers from the seventeenth century, and the census of 1703 is the oldest complete census of a whole country anywhere in the world. That habit now lives in a database carrying Ari’s title, where every Icelander can read the chain of names and dates from the settlement to himself.

So here is my line. First back to Snorri Sturluson, the author of the *Prose Edda* and *Heimskringla*. Then to Ingólfur Arnarson, by tradition the first man to settle Iceland, in 874. And then past the edge of the records, where *Íslendingabók* hands over to *Njála*, to Ragnar loðbrók.

### Rune by rune

{{< runetable >}}

Two small liberties: runic writing usually dropped doubled letters, so a carver would have written *Egertson*, and the dot on a stone would be punched into the stem rather than drawn beside it. I kept the doubled letters so that the name stays readable.

### A reading key

The sixteen runes of the Younger Futhark, in their traditional order, are *fé, úr, þurs, óss, reið, kaun, hagall, nauð, íss, ár, sól, Týr, bjarkan, maðr, lögr, ýr*. The Icelandic rune poem, preserved in manuscripts from the fifteenth century, gives each rune a short verse. The rune for wealth, *fé*, opens with the line "wealth is a source of discord among kinsmen." An economist could hardly ask for a better epigraph.
