The line at the top of every page is my name, **Gauti B. Eggertsson**, written in runes. Runes were the letters of Scandinavia before the Latin alphabet arrived with Christianity. They were carved rather than written, on stone, bone, and wood, which is why nearly every rune is built from straight strokes that cut easily across the grain.

When Iceland was settled by Norse men and women in the 870s, the alphabet in use across Scandinavia was the **Younger Futhark**, a compact set of sixteen runes that had taken shape around the year 800. Sixteen signs were not enough for every sound. One rune served for both *k* and *g*, one for *i* and *e*, one for *u* and *o*. Around the eleventh and twelfth centuries scribes began adding a small dot to a rune to mark the second sound, and these **dotted runes** became the standard medieval alphabet in Iceland and Norway. They survive on Icelandic gravestones and church objects well into the fifteenth century. The header uses this medieval form, because it is the one that can spell my name in full.

Two small liberties: runic writing usually dropped doubled letters, so a carver would have written *Egertson*, and the dot on a stone would be punched into the stem rather than drawn beside it. I kept the doubled letters so that the name stays readable.

### A reading key

The sixteen runes of the Younger Futhark, in their traditional order, are *fé, úr, þurs, óss, reið, kaun, hagall, nauð, íss, ár, sól, Týr, bjarkan, maðr, lögr, ýr*. The Icelandic rune poem, preserved in manuscripts from the fifteenth century, gives each rune a short verse. The rune for wealth, *fé*, opens with the line "wealth is a source of discord among kinsmen." An economist could hardly ask for a better epigraph.
